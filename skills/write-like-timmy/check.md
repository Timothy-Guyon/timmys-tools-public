# Checking the voice against his real writing

The rules in `voice.md` come from Timmy's reactions to drafts, and a set of rules can drift into a caricature of him without anyone noticing. This check asks one question: given the same content, is a draft written with the voice file closer to what Timmy really wrote than a draft written without it? It follows the test in Mike Taylor's "How I Finally Got AI to Write Like Me" (Every, 2026-11-16), where a style guide written from a reading of his posts made drafts less like him than no guide at all.

Where his private examples can be reached (`voice.py examples` prints some), it asks a second question too: is a draft written after reading his examples closer than a draft written with the rules alone? The rules alone are all a place on the public copy gets, such as his work laptop. So a gap here means the rules are missing something the examples show, and closing it with a rule brings every place up. In Timmy's words: "we can treat that as a signal that there's some gap that needs to be closed" (2026-11-23).

Run it only where his real writing can be read, and leave it there.

1. **Pick the pieces.** Eight to twelve that he wrote for people, not to an assistant, each at least two sentences long, none of them one of his examples (under "Examples" in `voice.md` or printed by `voice.py examples`) or the source of a rule. Messages, posts and documents all count; from a document take one section, not the whole. Pick across the kinds this place can see, and prefer recent ones.
2. **Write a brief for each:** what the piece had to carry (who it was for, what he wanted, the facts) and nothing of how he said it: none of his phrases, his order or his tone.
3. **Draft each from its brief alone,** each time in a fresh session or agent that has not seen the real piece: once with no voice file, once following `voice.md`, and, where there are examples, once following `voice.md` after reading his examples the way step 1 of `SKILL.md` says.
4. **Have a fresh judge compare two drafts at a time.** It gets the real piece and the two drafts, labeled 1 and 2 with nothing to say which is which, and answers which is closer to the real one in voice and, in a line, how each differs from it. Ask twice with the order swapped. A pair whose answer flips is a tie. Compare the draft with `voice.md` against the draft without it and, where there are examples, the draft that read them against the draft with `voice.md` alone.
5. **Record it:** `python3 scripts/voice.py check --with <n> --without <n> --ties <n> --note "<what the judge's notes say most>"`, adding `--with-examples <n> --rules-only <n> --examples-ties <n>` for the second comparison where there was one.
6. **Tell Timmy what it found,** in two lines. If drafts without the voice file were closer more often, a rule is pulling drafts away from him: use the judge's notes to find which, and put it to him. Where a rule pulls away from his past writing because he asked for the change (shorter at work, more shape in casual writing), the rule is doing its job; say so, and his rule stands. If the drafts that read his examples were closer more often, the rules are missing something the examples show: find it in the judge's notes, write it as a rule in general terms with made-up illustrations, never a line from an example, since `voice.md` is public, and put it to him.

The judge's notes on how a draft differs from the real piece are lessons like any other: one that repeats is a rule to propose, as "How the voice grows" in `SKILL.md` says.
